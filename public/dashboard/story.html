Thirty years of economies: no clean types, shared shocks for some kinds, and a catch-up that came and went

Draft v1, 14 October 2026. Every number below has an ID in square brackets that points to the claim, its evidence and its caveat on the methods page; quote a number only with that caveat. Data: World Bank World Development Indicators

In short

I wrote down four hypotheses before looking at any result. The groupings I tested were not stable enough to count as types [C11]. Recessions and debt jumps cluster in a few global years, and a smaller share of inflation-spike starts falls in the years my test flags [C15, C17, C32]. Poorer economies grew a little faster over the whole period; the estimate was strong in 2005 to 2014 and compatible with zero in 2015 to 2024 [C19, C20, C21]. For growth, no model I tried beat a five-year average [C26]. For inflation, a rule that picks the method from earlier years' errors does not beat "same as last year" [C31]. The model that did best on the test years does, by about 7%, but that is only known afterwards [C24]. Two hypotheses were rejected, one was confirmed but fragile and one was inconclusive. The sections below give the evidence, then what went wrong along the way and what the data cannot say.

What I looked at

I built a country-year panel for 217 economies (this includes territories and special regions, so "economy" fits better than "country") from 1995 to 2024, with eleven indicators. Population and life expectancy are close to complete [C1]; GDP growth, income per person and unemployment are available for at least 84% of economies in every year [C2]. Central government debt is mostly missing (15% to 26% of economies), so I use the IMF's general government debt instead. I stop in 2024 because inflation data for 2025 cover only 78% of economies [C3].

Missing data is mostly whole series that never start, start late or end early; gaps inside a series are rare [C4]. Pooling economies and years can mislead: 22 of 55 pairs of indicators change the sign of their correlation once each economy's own average is removed [C6]. Unusual year-on-year jumps concentrate in 2009 and 2020 to 2022 [C7]. That is a count, not a test of whether shocks are shared; section 2 runs the test.

Before looking at the results I wrote down four hypotheses and exactly how each could be proved wrong (docs/HYPOTHESES.md).

1. The groups I tested were not stable

I grouped economies by income, growth and its volatility, inflation, unemployment, government debt and the current account (2015-19, 163 economies with complete data). A clustering method always returns groups, so the question is whether they are real. They are weak: the silhouette score is about 0.18, only slightly better than the same data with each column shuffled [C10]. When I repeatedly resampled the economies, the groups agreed with the full-sample groups only moderately: an agreement score (the adjusted Rand index, where 1 is perfect) of 0.57 to 0.64, against a bar of 0.80 I set in advance [C11]. The bar is a choice, not a boundary between real and unreal groups. That did not change when I scaled the data differently, added economies with a few missing values, or used other random seeds [C11]. Only about half of the economies sit firmly in one group [C12]. Agreement between one five-year window and the next (0.04 to 0.42) is lower than between resamples within a window (about 0.57), but the samples differ, so I cannot say how much of the gap is real change [C13]. Hypothesis 1, that there are four to six stable types, is rejected.

I did not test for a continuum. Against a correlated normal cloud with the same covariance as the data, the groups' silhouettes were a little higher (0.18 to 0.19 against a 95th percentile of 0.17 to 0.18). I tried four to six groups and made no adjustment for that search, so I read this as weak structure, not as evidence of distinct types. The groups are unstable [C10].

2. Shocks are shared, but only for some kinds

Recessions cluster sharply in 2009 and 2020, and big jumps in government debt in 2009 and 2020 (and, less firmly, 1998), far more than shuffling the years within each economy would produce [C17]. That shuffle treats the years open to an economy as interchangeable, so it cannot allow for risk that changes over time. Inflation spikes are different. With the 1,000 shuffles I fixed in advance, only 2008 stands out, and it holds 15% of all spikes, well short of the majority hypothesis 2 required [C15]. The same holds whether spikes are defined loosely or strictly [C16]. A finer test with 10,000 shuffles also flags 1999 and 2022; the three years hold 36% of spikes, still short of a majority [C32]. Years close to the cut-off, such as 1999 and 2022, move in and out of the "shared" list depending on the number of shuffles and the random seed; 2008, 2009 and 2020 never do [C17]. Under these definitions, a smaller share of inflation-spike starts than of recessions or debt jumps falls in flagged years. Failing to flag a year is not evidence that its spikes were unshared, because the test has little power when counts are small.

A second method, change-point detection, agrees only in part. A third or fewer of inflation spikes and recessions, and about 70% of debt jumps, fall within a year of a detected change in an economy's average level [C18].

3. Catch-up was modest, patchy, and depended on the decade

Between 1995 and 2024, economies that started poorer grew a little faster on average: a slope of -0.42 points of annual growth per log unit of starting income (95% interval -0.61 to -0.23) [C19]. That average hides a lot. It was strong in 2005-2014 (-0.81) and indistinguishable from zero in 2015-2024 (-0.04) [C20]. Within regions it is clearest in Europe and Central Asia. Hypothesis 3, that convergence is visible within regions but weaker across the world, passes the test I wrote down, which uses unadjusted intervals, but it is not robust: it fails if the poorest 5% of economies are dropped or if four years are averaged at each end, and only one region survives a correction for testing five regions [C21]. The spread of incomes across economies narrowed only from 1.21 to 1.16 (standard deviation of log income), flat until about 2005 and widening again after 2020 [C22]. Growth is computed from the same starting income that predicts it, and errors may be correlated across economies, which robust standard errors do not address. "Poor countries are catching up" is too strong a slogan for these data.

4. The future is hard to forecast, and a trailing average is hard to beat

I then asked how well next year's inflation and growth can be forecast, comparing four simple baselines with Ridge and LightGBM models in a walk-forward test by observation year, over 20 test years (2005-2024). These are backtests on the latest data release, which includes revisions that forecasters did not have at the time. For growth, a plain five-year average had the lowest error (3.47 percentage points, against 3.52 for the best learned model) and about 11% lower than assuming next year equals this year (3.97) [C26]. For inflation, the best model, picked in hindsight on the test years (LightGBM, error 0.642 on a log-like scale), beat "next year equals this year" (0.691) by about 7% [C24], but a simple per-economy autoregression was close behind (0.657) [C25]. The hindsight pick flatters the models. A rule that chooses each year's method from the errors of earlier years, from 2008 on, had an inflation error of 0.746 against 0.721 for "next year equals this year" on the same forecasts, and for growth it did worse than the five-year average (3.92 against 3.60) [C31]. Hypothesis 5, that persistence is at least as accurate as the best model for inflation, is inconclusive: the model has the lower error in all four income groups, but the gain is clear in only two [C27]. The nominal 90% prediction intervals covered 86% to 88% of outcomes in total, and only 77% to 81% for growth in 2020-2024, and a possible reason is a shock unlike the years used to calibrate them, which I did not test [C28]. Both models depend mainly on an economy's own recent history [C30].

What went wrong along the way

Some of the mistakes would have changed the results had they stayed.

Several series hold exact zeros that look like missing values: Venezuela's exports and capital formation, Ethiopia's capital formation and the Republic of Congo's debt in the late 1990s [C34]. Zeros pass a range check. An exact-zero scan found them, and they are excluded and listed in docs/DATA_LOG.md.

My first rule for flagging unusual jumps called ordinary population growth an error. It now compares log changes for income and population.

The IMF's server refused scripted requests, so its debt series was loaded in a browser and saved by hand. I did not work around the block.

Two of my own expectations failed. I expected "same as last year" to be the strongest inflation baseline, and a per-economy autoregression beat it [C25]. I also took the World Bank's very negative inflation values at face value until the largest forecast errors pointed back at them. Of 16 values below -5% that the IMF also reports, six are strongly contradicted by the IMF's series [C29]. I left the data as published and say so. Dropping those rows does not change the verdict on hypothesis 5 [C27].

What I cannot say

  • Why any of this happened. These are descriptions of one panel, with no causal design.
  • Anything about the economies with too little data, which are mostly small ones [C14, C5].
  • That thresholds are right. Event definitions are arbitrary, which is why each is shown under three versions.
  • That the forecasts are fit for any decision about real people or places, or that a different model class would not do better (the list was short on purpose). See docs/MODEL_CARD.md.
  • How these forecasts would have done in real time. Data revisions and release delays were not modelled.
  • That the data are clean. Only inflation values below -5% were checked against the IMF [C29].

How this was made

Hypotheses were fixed before any analysis, and every rejection is kept. Each number was checked a second way before it was allowed into this draft: recomputed in SQL or with separate code, or compared with a second source. The claims register says which, and names any part that was only traced. A test rebuilds the whole panel from the raw downloads and checks that the outputs are identical. In the explorer every chart has a table of the same numbers and a link to its source. Method, decisions and limits are in docs/METHODOLOGY.md and docs/DECISIONS.md.
